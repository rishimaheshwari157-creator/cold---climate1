Development Log

Every prompt given to Claude and the analytical logic it added at each pipeline stage.

Prompt 1 — Market Database

"Build a global market growth database with weights for industrial IoT, premium positioning, EU-origin trade corridors, and asset-light constraints."

Logic added: A synthetic market database of 45 countries indexed by category-fit, GDP growth, trade openness, and regional cluster proximity. Weighting adjusted for home-country trade agreements so German-origin products score higher in Nordic and Central European corridors initially.

Prompt 2 — Weighting System

"Apply a profile-driven weighting system where 'low liability' and 'capital capped at €2M' penalize capital-heavy entry markets."

Logic added: Dynamic scoring matrix using free-text parsing. Keywords like "premium positioning" boost social/technological dimensions. "Low liability" lowers joint-venture weights. "Capital capped" suppresses high-CAPEX markets regardless of growth rate.

Prompt 3 — PESTEL Blend

"Generate PESTEL scores for the top 7 markets and blend them into a composite using weights derived from the business profile."

Logic added: Six-dimension PESTEL matrix per country. Blend weights are not static: Political = 15%, Economic = 20%, Social = 20%, Technological = 20%, Environmental = 10%, Legal = 15%. Weights shift ±5% based on keywords in constraints (e.g., "regulatory limits" bumps Legal to 25%).

Prompt 4 — Entry-Mode Scoring

"Score five entry modes for each of the top 3 countries using constraint alignment, strategic objective match, and profile fit."

Logic added: Mode scoring uses a multi-attribute utility model. Direct Export scores high on "low liability" but lower on local presence. Joint Venture scores high on strategic partnership but penalized by liability keywords. Licensing scores well on capital constraints. Subsidiary is heavily penalized by "capital capped." E-commerce is boosted by "asset-light."

Audit Bias Detection & Mitigation

Bias Audit Note

Method: The dashboard was tested against 10 diverse products: industrial IoT sensors (Germany), plant-based snacks (Brazil), B2B SaaS (Singapore), agricultural machinery (Canada), luxury apparel (Italy), renewable energy components (Japan), medical devices (USA), construction materials (UAE), consumer electronics (China), and educational software (Nigeria).

Bias detected (v0.1): The initial market database over-weighted "premium positioning" toward Western European countries. For luxury apparel from Italy, the top 7 included 4 EU countries. For agricultural machinery from Canada, the model still favored Northern Europe due to trade-corridor clustering, despite stronger growth in Southeast Asia for that category.

Fix applied: Introduced category-specific growth vectors (e.g., agriculture → Southeast Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa; electronics → East Asia and South Asia; SaaS → North America and Southeast Asia). Added a diversity factor in the blending algorithm: if 4 of 7 markets fall within the same geographic region, the lower-scored markets from different regions receive a +5% diversity multiplier, capped at three applications per pipeline.

Entry mode bias: "Joint Venture" was initially favored for all B2B products regardless of risk profile. The fix added a liability-sensitivity check: if the business profile explicitly mentions "low liability" or "low appetite for joint liability," the Joint Venture score is reduced by 30% and Licensing/E-commerce receive proportional boosts.

Outcome: Post-fix, cross-product tests show the top 3 markets include at least 2 different regions in 9 of 10 scenarios. Entry mode diversity improved: no single mode dominates across all categories. Confidence scoring was adjusted to reflect lower variance when diversity is high.

Presentation — Defending Design Choices

A 10–15 minute defense of the architecture, UX, analytical model, and ethical framework.

1. Why Free-Text Only? No Dropdowns.

Dropdowns constrain strategic thinking. A user selecting "Apparel" from a list does not reveal whether they mean premium fashion, protective gear, or fast fashion. Free-text input allows Claude to parse vocabulary, intent, and nuance—enabling dynamic category mapping rather than static buckets. This aligns with modern NLP capabilities and reduces user cognitive load.

2. Three-Stage Pipeline Design

The pipeline mirrors strategic consulting practice: (1) opportunity screening, (2) contextual filtering, (3) operational scoring. Separating market selection from entry-mode selection prevents premature optimization. Without PESTEL, you might select a high-growth market that is legally hostile to your capital constraints. The stages enforce discipline.

3. PESTEL Blend Weighting

Static weights treat every business identically. Our blend uses profile keywords to shift weights: legal constraints boost Legal; technological products boost Technological; capital limits boost Economic. This reflects the reality that strategic relevance is context-dependent, not universal.

4. Entry Mode Scoring Logic

We use a multi-attribute utility model rather than a simple checklist. Each mode—Direct Export, Joint Venture, Licensing, Subsidiary, E-commerce—is scored across capital intensity, liability exposure, strategic presence, and speed. The user profile acts as a filter: if liability is low, Joint Venture scores drop automatically. This prevents generic recommendations.

5. Bias Mitigation & Audit Trail

Bias is inevitable in training data and synthetic databases. Our response is transparency: a visible audit section, cross-product testing, and algorithmic fixes (diversity multipliers, liability-sensitive penalties). We do not claim neutrality; we claim accountability. The audit note explicitly states what was changed and why.

6. Visual Design & Trust

Dark, glassmorphic design with gold and cool-blue accents signals premium analysis without pretension. The typography uses a serif display for strategic authority and a sans-serif for data clarity. Progress rails, stage badges, and score bars provide continuous feedback, reducing uncertainty anxiety during multi-stage workflows.

7. Confidence, Not Certainty

We end with a confidence score, not a guarantee. This reflects epistemic humility. The score is derived from input specificity (more detail = higher confidence) and score variance across the top 3 (high variance = lower confidence in the leader). It teaches users to treat recommendations as strategic hypotheses, not commands.
